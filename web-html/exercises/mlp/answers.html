Mlp - Answers

  1. Why is composing simple functions a good strategy for nonlinear maps?
  • Can build complicated models out of simple ones
  • Gradient descent is easy to implement as long as the simple functions are differentiable
  • Parameters are never reused
  • Inefficient GPU implementations for simple differentiable functions

Explanation: When composing simple function there is a potential for parameter reuse depending on how the functions are composed. Moreover, there are efficient implementations for simple differentiable functions.

  1. For \(x \longmapsto z_1(x, \theta) \longmapsto z_2(z_1, \theta) \longmapsto ... \longmapsto z_M(z_{M-1}, \theta) \longmapsto f_\theta(x) \) which of the following terminology is accurate for the lecture?
  • \(x\) is a feature vector
  • \(\theta\) contains all parameters used across layers
  • \(z_i(z_{i-1}, \theta)\) is intermediate layer
  • \(z_i\) as a function is an intermediate feature
  • \(f_{\theta}(x)\) is the decision variable

Explanation: The numeric value \(z_i(z_{i-1}, \theta)\) is an intermediate feature, while \(z_i\) as a function is an intermediate layer. \(\theta\) contains all parameters used across layers, and each layer uses parts of \(\theta\) for computation. \(f_{\theta}(x)\) is the decision variable that can be used as is for numeric predictions or as a label for classification depending on use case.

  1. What are two key types of layers discussed for function composition?
  • Classification
  • Linear
  • Elementwise nonlinearity
  • Regression

Explanation: The main types of layers discussed in this class are linear and elementwise nonlinearity (or activations). Classification and regression are not layer types, they are problem types.

  1. For the following linear layer \(W \cdot z + b\), if the input shape of \(z\) is \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) and the output shape is \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\), what must be the shape of \(b\)?

(b) \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\)

Explanation: Since output shape is \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\), \(d_2 = 6\). Shape of \(b\) is always \(\mathbb{R}^{d_2}\), so it must be also \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\).
Another way to look at this is: the only way to get a vector of shape \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\) from a sum of two vectors is only if both vectors are also of shape \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\).

  1. For the following linear layer \(W \cdot z + b\), if the input shape of \(z\) is \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) and the output shape is \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\), what must be the shape of \(W\)?

(b) \(\mathbb{R}^{6 \times 3}\)

Explanation: Since input shape is \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\), \(d_1 = 3\). Since output shape is \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\), \(d_2 = 6\). Shape of \(W\) is always \(\mathbb{R}^{d_2 \times d_1}\), so it must be \(\mathbb{R}^{6 \times 3}\).
Another way to look at this is: the only way to get a vector of shape \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\) from a sum of two vectors is only if both vectors are also of shape \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\). So \(W \cdot z\) must have shape \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\). This means \(W\) must have 6 rows. For the multiplication \(W \cdot z\) to be valid, \(W\) must have same number of columns as number of rows of \(z\) which is 3. So \(W\) must have shape \(\mathbb{R}^{6 \times 3}\).

  1. For the following linear layer \(W \cdot z + b\), if the input shape of \(z\) is \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) and the output shape is \(\mathbb{R}^{6}\), how many parameters does the learning algorithm need to find?

(c) 24

Explanation: The parameters in this layer are \(W\) and \(b\). This means total number parameters depends on the shape of \(W\) and \(b\). Specifically, number of parameters are \(d_1*d_2 + d_2 = 3*6 + 6 = \) 24 where \(d_1*d_2\) is number of parameters that comes from W and \(d_2\) is number of parameters that comes from \(b\).

  1. Calculate \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}\).

Answer: \(\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 15 \\ 22 \end{bmatrix}\)

Explanation:

\(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \\ 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 2 \\ 5 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} \\ = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 15 \\ 22 \end{bmatrix}\).

  1. Calculate \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}\).

Answer: \(\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 15 \\ 22 \end{bmatrix}\)

Explanation:
\(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 \\ 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 1 \\ 5 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} \\ = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 15 \\ 22 \end{bmatrix}\).

Note: adding 1 helps omit separate bias and formulate linear layer as just matrix-vector multiplication: \(\begin{pmatrix} W & b \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\)

  1. How to convert \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 5\) into specifically \(W \cdot z + b\) format (where \(W\) is a matrix and \(z, b\) are vectors)?
  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 5\)
  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 5\)
  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + 5\)
  • \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\)

Explanation: \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is equivalent to \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) as they both produce value \(3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 2\). So \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 5 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 5\).

Note: Dot-product layer with scalar output \(a \cdot z + b\) is just a special case of a linear layer and can be represented in the original format \(W \cdot z + b\) by just transposing vector \(a\).

  1. Does composing two linear layers introduce additional complexity to the learned model?
    (b) No, composing two linear layers is equivalent to a single linear layer

Explanation: As discussed in the lecture, \(x \longmapsto W_1 x \longmapsto W_2 (W_1 x)\) is equivalent to \(x \longmapsto W x\) where \(W = W_2 \cdot W_1\). This means that composing two linear layers is same as having one linear layer.

Note: this is why just linear layers are not enough and we need elementwise nonlinearity to produce more interesting results.

  1. How many hyperparameters and learnable parameters does the following activation layer have \(\begin{bmatrix} e^{z_0} \\ e^{z_1} \\ e^{z_2} \end{bmatrix}\) where \(z = \begin{bmatrix} z_0 \\ z_1 \\ z_2 \end{bmatrix}\)?

(d) 0 hyperparameters and 0 learnable parameters

Explanation: There are no hyperparameters or learnable parameters in this layer: given intermediate feature vector \(z\) we can calculate the elementwise nonlinearity.

  1. Which criteria are considered when choosing an activation function?
  • Smooth functions help with gradient descent on the loss function
  • Vanishing derivatives are good for finishing learning fast with good quality output
  • Linear activation is good for its smoothness and simplicity
  • Complicated functions can be bad for their computational expense

Explanation:
Smooth functions are important during training for using gradient descent, so choosing smooth activation functions can help with gradient descent on the loss function.
Vanishing derivatives are bad for training using gradient descent. Specifically, because the derivative is so close to 0, which means the gradient is close to 0, the descent part of gradient descent does not happen and the algorithm gets stuck.
While linear layer is smooth and simple, as discussed in problem 10, it is not a good activation function because it does not introduce more complexity/nonlinearity.
Computing derivatives of complicated functions can be computationally expensive, making training a model with these layers too expensive to be realistic.

  1. What is special about the \(softmax\) function as an activation function?
  • Has multiple hyperparameters
  • Has multiple learnable parameters
  • Nothing special
  • Not-just-elementwise nonlinearity

Explanation: Remember that \(softmax(z)_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^m e^{z_j}}. \\\) \(softmax\) activation function does not introduce any hyperparameters or learnable parameters, but it is not just elementwise nonlinearity because we use all elements to compute \(softmax\) for each element.

  1. What is the main difference between a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) and a feedforward neural network (FNN)?
  • MLP has linear layers, while FNN does not
  • MLP has elementwise activations, while FNN does not
  • MLP has both linear layers and elementwise activations, while FNN does not
  • The two are the same

Explanation: MLP and FNN are different names for the same concept: composition of functions such that we interleave linear layers with elementwise activations!

  1. If an MLP has 1 layer of size 4, its input is in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\), what is the total number of learnable parameters?

Answer: 12.

Explanation: MLP with one layer is equivalent to \(x \longmapsto \sigma_1 (W_1 \cdot x + b_1)\). Therefore, the total number of learnable parameters is number of parameters from \(W_1\) + number of parameters from \(b_1\). We know that x \(\in \mathbb{R}^{2}\) and layer size is 4, so according to our notation \(d_0 = 2, d_1 = 4\). Since \(W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_0}\) and \(b_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1}\), \(W_1\) has \(d_1*d_0 = 4*2 = 8\) parameters and \(b_1\) has \(d_1 = 4\) parameters. So this MLP has 8+4 = 12 learnable parameters

  1. If an MLP has 3 layers each of size 4, its input is in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\), what is the total number of learnable parameters?

Answer: 52.

Explanation:
MLP with 3 layers is equivalent to \(x \longmapsto z_1 = \sigma_1 (W_1 \cdot x + b_1) \longmapsto z_2 = \sigma_2 (W_2 \cdot z_1 + b_2) \longmapsto \sigma_3 (W_3 \cdot z_2 + b_3)\).
All the learnable parameters come from \(W_1, b_1, W_2, b_2, W_3, b_3\).
We know that x \(\in \mathbb{R}^{2}\) and layer size is 4, so according to our notation \(d_0 = 2, d_1 = d_2 = d_3 = 4\).
Since \(W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_0}\), \(b_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1}\), \(W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_2 \times d_1}\), \(b_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_2}\), \(W_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_3 \times d_2}\), \(b_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_3}\),
\(W_1\) has \(d_1*d_0 = 4*2 = 8\) parameters, \(b_1\) has \(d_1 = 4\) parameters,
\(W_2\) has \(d_2*d_1 = 4*4 = 16\) parameters, \(b_2\) has \(d_2 = 4\) parameters,
\(W_3\) has \(d_3*d_2 = 4*4 = 16\) parameters, and \(b_3\) has \(d_3 = 4\) parameters.
So this MLP has 8 + 4 + 16 + 4 + 16 + 4 = 52 parameters.

  1. Evaluate MLP for feature vector \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\), where MLP has following hyperparameters: \(L = 2, d_0=2, d_1=3, d_2=3\), \(W_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, b_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, W_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, b_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\) using ReLU activations for all layers.

Answer: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\)

Explanation:
\(L = 2\) means there are 2 layers. MLP with 2 layers is equivalent to \(x \longmapsto z_1 = ReLU(W_1 \cdot x + b_1) \longmapsto ReLU(W_2 \cdot z_1 + b_2)\).
\(z_1 = ReLU(W_1 \cdot x + b_1) = ReLU(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}) = ReLU(\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}) = ReLU(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\).

\(z_2 = ReLU(W_2 \cdot z_1 + b_2) = ReLU(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}) = ReLU(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\).

So the result is \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\).

  1. What are the advantages and disadvantages of increasing the width vs. the depth of MLP?
  • Increasing depth (number of layers) can lead to vanishing gradients, while increasing width (size of a layer) can lead to large weight matrices
  • Increasing depth (size of a layer) can lead to vanishing gradients, while increasing width (number of layers) can lead to large weight matrices
  • More depth (number of layers) leads to a hierarchical structure, while a bigger width (size of a layer) compared to depth can lead to simpler training
  • More depth (size of a layer) leads to a hierarchical structure, while a bigger width (number of layers) compared to depth can lead to simpler training

Explanation: Depth is number of layers and width is a size of a layer. Increasing width means increasing size of a layer which means increasing the size of weight matrix \(W\). so if we increase the width a lot it can lead to large weight matrices. On the other hand, because these matrices used in linear layers increasing width compared to depth can lead to simpler training. Specifically, increasing depth would involve having more non-linear functions and therefore increasing difficulty of training. Increasing depth can also lead to vanishing gradients because if any of the nonlinearities in any layer vanishes the whole gradient vanishes. But increasing depth can also lead to hierarchical structure where each layer is "responsible" for different parts of a task (more on this during CNN discussion in future lectures).

  1. Which of the following are true about Multilayer Perceptrons?
  • Gradient descent can no longer be used for training MLP models
  • MLPs are computationally expensive to train
  • For a wide class of functions f(x), can get arbitrarily close to the f(x) function f_\theta(x) with just an MLP
  • Additional training techniques like Dropout and Batch normalization can help reduce overfitting and speed up training, respectively

Explanation:
Gradient descent can still be used to train MLPs because we can still define a loss that measure how well it performs.
MLPs can be computationally expensive to train, especially if width or depth is too large.
There are theoretical and practical proofs (that you are not expected to know) showing that continuous functions are well-approximated by MLPs with any nonpolynomial activation function (might not find right parameters during training but they exist).
Dropout can empirically improve overfitting, and batch normalization can empirically stabilize/accelerate training.
